Collapsed Possibilities

A new end

19 October 2026 Pescara, Italy

Research is one of those things that never had a clear beginning for me. I have thought about it ever since I understood what a university was and what academia meant. Through ups and downs on that idea, I did my bachelor’s, then my master’s.

Then came Easter 2020. Covid had shut everything down, and for weeks I had been closed in a shared room, the days all alike and all spent, happily, on my master’s thesis (whose topic, I confess, I did not like at first). One evening, at the end of a call, my thesis supervisor at the time offered me a PhD. Reality collapsed. The rest of the story is on the main page of this site, which is why the site was born in the first place: to host a CV. What that page does not say is how this part of the story ends: I am leaving academia.

It is a decision that grew out of ideas spinning in my head for a long time, and writing here, for me, is a way to line them up. Making them public, even if nobody reads them, makes them real. Not that reality needs my help: it has already happened, and the possibilities have collapsed into the single reality of the tangent universe.

To explain why I am leaving, I need to say one thing about myself first. After changing my mind many times, I have come to consider myself a hedonist. I will not go into what I think about life, but over the years I have distilled it into one sentence: life is too short not to pursue pleasure and happiness. I know, put like that it carries at least six implications that need clarifying. Sorry, they will have to wait. Take it as it is.

Some will call this view selfish. But society is, in the end, an emergent property of individuals, each moving forward for their own survival. Character shapes how each of us does it, yet underneath there is always that same drive, and through the magic of complex systems what emerges is society, altruism included. I will not wade any deeper into complex systems, let alone their philosophical implications, which I could not untangle properly. I am an engineer, after all (stereotype included :-) ). For those who want to, and who respect my paradigm of the prerequisites for talking about these things (maybe a short rant post on that someday), let’s talk about it over a beer one evening. I look forward to hearing what you think, if we have not done it already.

Back to the point: this way of being is what led me to research. Civil engineering came in sideways, along a path shaped by other things, and the two converged at the same point in space-time. And I did find pleasure in that work: not always, as with everything in life, but the overall balance of these years is clearly positive. Richard Feynman called it the pleasure of finding things out. I would not dream of comparing myself to him, but the phrase has stayed with me.

Yet, on the verge of turning 33, after a PhD and three years as a postdoc, this phase has come to an end, and my path is turning elsewhere. It happens. It has happened to many people better than me (unfortunately), and we just hear less about them: academia has its own survivorship bias (and a stochastic term that those who made it tend to drop from the equation, consciously or not: a linear brain easily mistakes luck for merit). This time it is happening to me.

I regret nothing: not what I did, not the opportunities I let go, not the choices I made. Regret needs a counterfactual to compare against, and I only have a sample size of one. What I do feel is gratitude, for all the opportunities I have had in my life, and not only in academia. This is not the place to thank everyone who, in one way or another, for a moment short or long, has been beside me in whatever role. As I write this, they are all passing through my mind, and I am struck by how many there are.

And I am not leaving empty-handed. I take with me a shape for the doubts and questions I have always had (since childhood, or so my family tells me, poor them; luckily I do not remember, and by my early teens I had learned to channel that side of me into something more productive). I take with me the ability to break down, on my own, a project that looks enormous, something I struggled with at first (the line between that and salami publication is razor-thin, and the more scientific literature I read, the thinner it gets). And I take with me the ability to work with many people very different from me, through discussions that were sometimes heated, without losing my temper. What helped was never losing sight of the final goal. Not a recipe, though: it got me through the discussions, not through academia.

What comes next

What does tomorrow hold? The details are a conversation for that beer. The broad picture: a lot of uncertainty, surely. But also many good things, as many have happened in the past (though extrapolation beyond the calibration range in a non-stationary process is optimism, not prediction). We move through the present knowing only the past that has come true, plus a few expectations drawn from the patterns we extrapolate into the future. That is what uncertainty is: the way the human brain perceives the infinite possibilities that have not yet come true. Some of them depend on us, in the sense that we choose to push in one direction. Then again, a push is not a trajectory: between the two lie inertia and boundary conditions, and reality does not always comply. But the possibilities are many.

Research never had a clear beginning for me; at least it has a clear end. Today the lines converge on a single point once more, and from here they fan out again: a new end, which from this side looks a lot like a beginning. Until reality collapses them again, I like to close my eyes and enjoy the possibilities. For once, I am in no hurry.
