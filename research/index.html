My research in plain words

What I study, explained without the jargon

I study how structures respond to earthquakes and other extreme events, and how to make existing ones safer. This page explains what my papers are about for anyone curious, with no engineering background needed. Each summary links to the full paper.

Making older buildings safer, at a sensible cost

Most buildings in use today were built before modern earthquake rules. Strengthening all of them is not possible, so the question becomes where to act and how, to get the most safety from every euro spent.

6 papers

Instead of only asking whether an older building passes a safety check, this method estimates how rarely earthquakes would push it past given levels of damage, including collapse. It then looks for the strengthening solutions that meet the target at the lowest cost.

Probabilistic Engineering Mechanics, 2025 →

Strengthening a building against earthquakes is often designed through experience and trial and error. This review surveys more structured methods that also weigh costs and environmental and social impacts, compares their strengths and limits, and points out what research still has to solve.

Bulletin of Earthquake Engineering, 2025 →

Many existing buildings stand on masonry walls with no reinforcement, which makes them vulnerable to earthquakes. This study presents a computer method that chooses which walls to strengthen with reinforced plaster, so that the building passes its safety checks at the lowest cost.

Bulletin of Earthquake Engineering, 2024 →

Strengthening a building costs money today, but earthquake damage costs money in the future too. This study uses artificial intelligence to find strengthening solutions that are cheap to build and also keep the expected yearly cost of damage under control.

Computers and Structures, 2022 →

Old concrete columns can fail in an earthquake in different ways, some more sudden than others. This study improves a computer method that finds the cheapest way to strengthen them with steel, whichever kind of weakness they have.

Engineering Structures, 2021 →

Many older concrete buildings were not designed for earthquakes, and strengthening them is expensive. This study uses a computer method inspired by natural evolution to work out which columns need steel reinforcement, and how much, so that safety is reached at a lower cost.

Engineering Structures, 2020 →

How structures behave, and how to watch over them

To protect a structure, you first need to understand how it reacts when things go wrong: strong shaking, a lost column, a collision. Part of this work also looks at how to notice damage as it happens.

6 papers

In an earthquake, a concrete column can bend in any direction, and how far it can bend before failing depends on that direction. Using a large set of simulated column sections and machine learning, this study derives compact formulas that engineers can use directly in their analysis and design tools.

Engineering Structures, under review →

What happens when a bridge pier is hit near its base, as in a collision with a vehicle? Scale-model columns made of steel tubes filled with concrete were struck by a hammer in the laboratory, with and without a support at the top. The support reduced the maximum sideways movement by 28-41%, but concentrated the damage near the point of impact.

Engineering Structures, under review →

During an earthquake, the masonry walls inside a concrete frame push against the columns and add extra force at their ends. This study provides a straightforward correction that lets engineers estimate this force while still using simple building models.

Journal of Structural Engineering, 2025 →

Can a masonry building tell us when it is getting damaged? Small sensors were placed in the mortar of test walls, which were then deliberately damaged in the laboratory, to develop an approach for monitoring a structure's health in real time.

Construction and Building Materials, 2024 →

If a column of a concrete building is suddenly lost, can the rest of the structure hold, or does the collapse spread? This study looks at how the masonry walls inside the frame influence the outcome.

Structures, 2023 →

The masonry walls that fill the frames of concrete buildings can be pushed outwards by an earthquake. This study offers a simple formula, based on laboratory tests and computer simulations, to estimate how strong these walls are against that push.

Engineering Structures, 2022 →

What happens in the ground

An earthquake starts deep underground, and the soil it crosses changes what reaches the surface. Places close to each other can shake very differently.

2 papers

To predict how shaking will vary across a town, engineers first need realistic earthquake motions for the deep rock beneath it. Using the town of Norcia and the 2016 Central Italy earthquake as a test, this study shows that motions generated on a computer can match real measurements more closely than the usual approach of scaling up past earthquakes.

Buildings, under review →

The ground under a building can change how an earthquake is felt at the surface. This study uses records from instruments placed below ground to fine-tune a computer model of the soil, and to measure how much uncertainty remains, for more reliable predictions of the shaking.

Soil Dynamics and Earthquake Engineering, 2026 →

New materials and structures

Some of my work with colleagues goes beyond traditional construction, from concrete made with recycled bamboo to 3D-printed metal lattices.

2 papers

Can waste from bamboo manufacturing make concrete greener? This study replaces part of the sand in a lightweight foamed concrete with recycled bamboo, and tests its strength, its heat and sound performance, and its environmental impact. The result is a promising low-density material that insulates better against heat.

Materials and Structures, 2026 →

Most materials get thinner when stretched, but auxetic structures do the opposite. This study 3D-prints stainless-steel lattices of this kind and adjusts their geometry so that they absorb more energy when crushed, which is useful for protective applications.

What I am working on lately

My starting question was a practical one: how do you make an existing building safe while spending as little as possible? Over the years that question has widened in three directions.

From cost to environmental impact. Money is not the only price of strengthening a building: materials and construction also have an environmental footprint. My recent work looks for solutions that balance both.

From one building to a whole region. Detailed computer models are too slow to study every building in an area. I am interested in simpler models that keep what matters, so that seismic risk can be assessed at the scale of a region.

From computer tools to AI agents. I have long used algorithms to search for good designs. Now AI agents can carry out entire parts of a research or engineering project, and I want to understand what this means for how reliable engineering decisions are, and who is accountable for them. I have been working on this hands-on for several months, since moving to Pescara. For a more organized account of why, see my notes from ECEE 2026 and other posts on my blog.
